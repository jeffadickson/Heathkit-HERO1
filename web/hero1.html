1 · What it was

A real robot, from the people who sold you an oscilloscope kit

At a December 1982 press conference Heath introduced HERO 1 as the nation's first mass-produced robot with a full set of senses. Reporters reached for the obvious comparison and said it looked like R2-D2. It could see light, hear sound, detect motion, range objects by sonar, talk through a phoneme synthesizer, roll around on three wheels and pick things up with a five-axis arm. Everything was run by an onboard Motorola 6808 that you programmed in hex on a keypad on its head.

Like every Heathkit, it came two ways. The ET-18 was a box of parts and a 213-page assembly manual; Heath estimated 40 to 60 hours of work and advised beginners not to try it. The ETW-18 arrived wired and tested, with the arm and voice included. Heath kept supporting the HERO family until 1995, and about 14,000 HERO 1s were sold.

On the sales number

Wikipedia and most retellings say 14,000 HERO 1s over eight years. The long-running owners' FAQ phrases the same figure as "14,000 assembled Hero 1's," which would put kits on top of it. Treat 14,000 as the best available total and the kit/wired split as unknown.

2 · Origins

How a kit company ended up building robots

The robot came out of Heath's education business rather than its hobby kits. Lou Frenzel had come to Heath from McGraw-Hill's home-study business in the early 1970s to start a line of self-instruction courses, each paired with a kit trainer you built and experimented on. The first products shipped in 1974. Microprocessor courses followed, built around the ET-3400 trainer: a 6800 on a board with a hex keypad and six LED digits. Frenzel later wrote that he helped conceive HERO and that his team designed and built it.

Seen from there, HERO 1 is an ET-3400 that grew wheels. It used the same processor family, the same keypad-and-seven-segment monitor and the same teaching method, and it shipped with a robotics course written by the same group. Heath was betting on industrial automation: robot counts in US plants were expected to grow more than tenfold during the decade, and those plants would need trained technicians.

The timing is notable. Heath started the project in October 1979, the same year Zenith bought the company from Schlumberger, the oil-services firm that also owned Fairchild Semiconductor. The block diagram in the schematic set carries a 1980 Heath copyright, so the electrical design was largely done two years before launch. The hard part was mechanical. A later education product manager recalled that Heath had exactly two mechanical engineers on staff, and Frenzel singled out the arm as the toughest part of the job. Owners' repair notes about loose wrists and binding heads bear that out.

The line outlived the company that made it. As Heath wound down in the 1990s it sold the HERO rights, documentation and parts to Mobile Ed Productions of Redford, Michigan. In 2002 Robert Doerr's Robot Workshop bought them and still supports HERO owners with parts and repairs.

3 · Who used it

Training rooms first, hobbyists second

Heath said plainly at launch that industry was the first target and the home would come later. HERO 1 was mainly a teaching instrument, and the evidence of its use comes from four directions.

  • Industrial and vocational training. The companion course (Robotics and Industrial Electronics) ran twelve lessons, about 120 hours, from robot fundamentals and fluid power through the 6808, sensors, speech and industrial robots, with an optional final exam graded by Heath. Creative Computing's 1983 review described it as built for industrial training as well as individuals. Heath's education group sold through representatives who called on technical schools.
  • Colleges and universities. CUNY's New York City College of Technology kept a HERO teaching students for about fifteen years before a student club revived it in 2017. The University of Wollongong wrote a 1984 working paper on loading HERO programs from Unix. A 1985 University of Central Florida master's thesis built a high-level language and compiler for it, and a 1987 Korean paper did the same on an Apple II. Czech Technical University rebuilt one with a PC/104 controller in 2007. One professor used his in embedded-systems classes because students could see their code move something, and blocked off the stairwells because the robot sometimes headed for them.
  • Schools and teachers. Individual teachers built them. A COMPUTE! columnist taught a 1983 robotics course in England on a HERO borrowed from Zenith Data Systems, and a former teacher remembers programming one to play Red Light, Green Light with kindergartners.
  • Hobbyists and television. Owners formed a mailing list and a user group that still trade parts and upgrades. HERO appeared on several episodes of Mr. Wizard's World, which is how many people first saw a robot that was not a movie prop.
What the record does not show

No source gives a count of school or college installations, and nothing I found documents military use. The usage picture above is assembled from individual institutions and reviews.

5 · The chip

The Motorola 6800 family

Motorola announced the MC6800 in March 1974, a few weeks before Intel's 8080. The chief architect was Tom Bennett, working with a team that included Jeff LaVell, Mike Wiles and Rod Orgill; Chuck Peddle joined in 1973 to take it to customers. It was an n-channel MOS part of about 4,000 transistors that ran at up to 1 MHz. Its most practical advantage over the 8080 was power: it needed only a single +5 V supply, while the 8080 needed three.

The architecture is clean and very readable. Two 8-bit accumulators, A and B, a 16-bit index register X, a 16-bit stack pointer, a 16-bit program counter and a condition-code register. It has 72 instructions in seven addressing modes, encoded in 197 opcodes, with memory-mapped I/O and no separate I/O space. Anyone who later met the 6502, 6809, 68HC11 or even the 68000 will recognize the style.

The chip that spawned the 6502

The 6800's most famous descendant is not a Motorola part. Launch pricing was $360, matching the 8080. In August 1974 Chuck Peddle left for MOS Technology in Pennsylvania, and seven Motorola engineers followed. There they built the 6501, which plugged into a 6800 socket, and the 6502, which had its own clock generator. Neither ran 6800 software. Selling for $20 to $25, they forced Motorola to cut the 6800 from $175 to $69 in October 1975. Motorola sued; in the April 1976 settlement MOS dropped the 6501, and the 6800 fell to $35. The cheap 6502 went on to the Apple II, the Commodore PET and the Atari 400/800.

Why HERO has a 6808

In 1977 Motorola released the MC6802, a 6800 with an on-chip clock oscillator and 128 bytes of RAM. The MC6808 is the same die with the RAM disabled, which let Motorola sell parts whose internal RAM had failed test. For Heath this was ideal. One crystal replaced the 6800's separate two-phase clock chip, and the robot needed external RAM anyway. The crystal is 3.579545 MHz, the NTSC color-burst frequency and the cheapest crystal in the world in 1980, divided by four internally for a bus of about 895 kHz. Heath's courses and the ET-3400 trainer already taught the 6800, so the robot course could build on lessons students had already taken.

The 6800 also left something useful unused. 197 of 256 opcodes are defined, which leaves 59 holes. HERO's Robot Interpreter fills those holes with robot commands. See chapter 7.

Other 6800-family machines include the Altair 680, the SWTPC 6800 (the most successful 6800 personal computer), the Sphere, the Tektronix 4051 graphics computer, the HP 9815A, the APF MP1000 console, and many arcade and pinball boards.

6 · Hardware

Fifteen boards in a fiberglass shell

HERO 1 is a bus-and-backplane machine folded into a robot. The CPU and I/O boards sit in the head next to the keypad and display. The power supply, drive electronics and batteries live in the base, and the boards are connected by ribbon cables through the rotating neck. Heath numbered every component by the board it sits on, so a part's reference designator tells you where to look.

What the ROM revealed

An 8 KB dump of the system ROM (U418) turned up in a 1991 collection of PC-era ROM images, and it runs. It identifies itself on the display as HEro 1.0, the original firmware for the 3.58 MHz crystal. The bytes the FAQ uses to tell the later 1.3 and 1.U upgrades apart match neither. Running it against the schematics answered questions the manuals leave open.

SubsystemWhat the firmware does
InterruptsA single IRQ handler at $EFC9 reads the eight flip-flops at $C200 and dispatches: sonar echo (bit 0), motion (1), motor battery low (2), logic battery low (3), the 1,024 Hz clock tick (4), pendant trigger (5), drive encoder (6), experimental board (7). Motion, trigger and experimental-board interrupts go through the RAM vectors at $0027, $002A and $002D. Writing $C200 both clears flags and sets the enable mask.
Everything else is the clock tickMotor stepping, the drive ramp and speech all run inside the 1,024 Hz tick, which is why the interpreter can start a motor and carry on with the next command.
SteppersTwo-phase bipolar sequence 0110 → 0101 → 1001 → 1010. Steering uses the top nibble of $C260; the other six motors share three nibbles ($C260 low, $C280 high and low), and bits 6 and 7 of $C2C0 choose which motor of each pair is energized. That multiplexing is why the interpreter moves only one arm motor at a time.
Gear ratiosOne position unit is 32 phase steps for extend, shoulder and head; 8 for wrist rotate, pivot and steering; 4 for the gripper.
Drive$C2A0 bit 7 sets direction, bit 6 switches the motor on, and bits 0–5 are PWM speed. The ROM ramps speed one level every 32 ms toward the target, then counts encoder interrupts down to zero and stops.
HomingInitialize drives each arm axis its full range by step count and lets it stall against the mechanical stop. It does not wait for a switch. Head and steering use their limit switches on $C260.
Keypad and displayThe ET-3400 design reused: keys pull one of five data lines low on rows read at $C003, $C005 and $C006; each digit is a 74LS259 written one segment at a time at $C11x–$C16x. The firmware also holds a table of spoken phrases, such as the one at $FA93 that the manual's speech example calls.

7 · Programming

Hex on the head, or a pendant in your hand

  • Keypad and monitor. From the executive ("ready") mode, press 1 for Machine Language or A for Robot Language. In the monitor, A autoloads (type an address, then two digits per byte), E examines, F and B step forward and back, C changes a byte, and D runs ("do") from an address. Keys 1–4 show A, B, PC and X.
  • Teaching pendant. A hand box with a motor selector, direction buttons and a trigger. In learn mode the robot records each motion and replays it. The trigger is also an interrupt your programs can use.
  • Robot Interpreter. The command language described below.
  • Host computer. Later options added RS-232, a memory board and a BASIC ROM. Third parties offered compilers for the IBM PC and Apple II: ANDROTEXT, Dunfield's C and several university languages.

The trick inside the Robot Interpreter

Heath did not invent a separate bytecode. The interpreter treats the 59 opcodes Motorola left undefined as robot commands, and it executes every ordinary 6800 instruction as well. You switch into it with SWI (3F) and back to native code with 83, so one program can mix both. The cost is speed: each interpreted instruction takes several hundred extra cycles. The manual's own benchmark runs a 6,000-pass delay loop in about 41 seconds in Robot Language and 0.3 seconds in Machine Language.

Every motor command packs motor, speed, direction and a 10-bit position into one 16-bit word.

1514131211109876543210 motorspeedDposition (abs) or distance (rel)

Motor: 0 drive, 1 extend, 2 shoulder, 3 wrist rotate, 4 wrist pivot, 5 gripper, 6 head, 7 steering. Speed: 01 slow, 10 medium, 11 fast. D = 0 runs toward a higher position number, D = 1 toward a lower one; for the drive motor it means forward or reverse. Head to $0BE at medium is 110 10 0 0010111110 = $D0BE, so the instruction is CC D0 BE. The interpreter moves only one arm or head motor at a time and queues the rest.

ROM services

The monitor ROM exports a handful of display and keypad routines that programs call with JSR, in the same style as the ET-3400. OUTCH and OUTSTR take raw segment patterns rather than ASCII: bit 6 is segment a through bit 0 for g, and bit 7 is the decimal point, which OUTSTR also uses to mark the end of a string. "H" is $37.

8 · Emulator

Run HERO 1 in the browser

Load Heath's original system ROM and this is a HERO 1: a 6808 running the 1982 firmware against emulated keypad, display latches, steppers, drive encoder, sonar, sense board, clock and SC-01. It boots to HEro 1.0 and says "ready". Pick a program and press Type it for me to watch the key sequence an owner would enter, or drive the keypad yourself: 1 (Machine) or A (Robot), then D and the four-digit start address. To key in your own code: 1, A, an address, then the bytes. Without a ROM file the page falls back to a JavaScript stand-in for the firmware.

Keypad layout follows Heath's block diagram. ABORT halts and shows the program counter.

How faithful is it?

The CPU is a complete MC6800 core: all 197 opcodes, Motorola's cycle counts and condition codes, checked against 200,000 random instructions on Joe Allen's EXORsim. The only differences were three places where EXORsim departs from Motorola's Programming Reference Manual.

With the ROM loaded, everything a HERO program sees is Heath's own code: the executive, monitor, keypad debounce, display routines, Robot Interpreter, motor ramps, speech sequencing and interrupt dispatch. The emulator only supplies the hardware. Its behavior was recovered from the schematics, the FAQ port map and the ROM itself: which keypad line each key pulls low, how the 74LS259 latches map to segments, the stepper phase sequences and gear ratios, the drive's PWM and ramp, the eight interrupt flip-flops at $C200, the SC-01 request handshake, and the MSM5832 clock, which reads your computer's time.

Physical scales are estimates: inches per drive encoder count, the sonar's range units, motor speeds on the floor, and the steering geometry. The robot starts as if it had been initialized earlier, because HERO's RAM survives sleep. Press INITIALIZE to watch the ROM home every axis.

The ROM file is not part of this page. Heath released the HERO ROMs for not-for-profit use by owners; load your own copy (an 8 KB dump of U418, SHA-1 4c5f5a06…661d for version 1.0). The page keeps it in this browser only.

9 · The family

What came after

HERO 1 1982

The educational original. 6808, 4 KB RAM, arm, sonar, SC-01 voice. About 14,000 sold.

HERO Jr. 1984

A home robot from Heath's consumer division with a preprogrammed personality: songs, games, guard duty, alarm clock. 6808, 2 KB RAM, 32 KB ROM, no arm. About 4,000 sold.

HERO 2000 1986

An 8088 master with up to eleven slave processors, BASIC in 64 KB of ROM, a card cage, 360° sonar, an optional arm and a floppy drive. About 3,000 sold.

HE-RObot 2007

A revival built with White Box Robotics on a Windows XP PC. Up to $8,000; about 50 sold before Heathkit's 2012 bankruptcy.
